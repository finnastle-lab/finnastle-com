Suzanne Pope wrote *An Inconvenient Truth for Copywriters* around 2010. Group creative director at john st. in Toronto, seventy-one pages, forty-odd ads, every agency credited. It's a craft document. A reminder to be a great writer, illustrated by people who were.

It gets described as a warning about copywriters going extinct. That's Part One, and she calls Part One "yet another lecture" herself and tells you to scroll past it if you'd rather.

Part Two is the value. The tools of rhetoric: metaphor, personification, hyperbole, irony, paradox, puns, parallelism, anesis, simile, the rhetorical question. Two thousand years old and still sharp. Practise them the way a musician practises scales.

The best line in it isn't hers. She takes a layout with a bad pun on it to Jerry Goodis, her first boss, and he hands it straight back. *If you're going to do a pun, it has to kick both ways.*

I've had that document on my drive for years. I've only just worked out what was bothering me about it.

## Look at what she chose

Nearly every ad in it is a visual idea.

The headline and the picture are doing it together, and neither half survives the other being taken away. A noisy child drawn as a loudspeaker. A bathroom fixture with a shocked face. She is cataloguing writing, and doing it almost entirely with work where the words and the image are welded together.

That's the job. It was the job long before she wrote. Bernbach sat the writer and the art director at the same desk in the sixties, and we have spent sixty years in pairs arguing about whether the line or the picture gets to carry it. Which is a different problem from words going extinct. It's a seating arrangement.

## The device is a slot

You can name the figure of speech in every one of those forty ads. Anesis. Paradox. Parallelism. Naming it tells you nothing about why the ad is good.

You could brief any of those structures to ten writers. All ten would hand back the structure, and nine would hand back nothing.

The device is a slot. What goes in the slot is the entire job.

## Special Buys

Not "weekly deals". Not "limited offers".

[*Special Buys.*](https://www.aldi.com.au/special-buys) Faintly wrong. Slightly foreign. "Buys" as a noun, which it barely is. And now load-bearing for a whole supermarket's personality in this country, to the point where people say it out loud in the aisle without embarrassment.

Nobody arrived at that by selecting a figure of speech.

CBA built years of work on *can* (M&C Saatchi, 2012). A verb, hung out on its own and used as a thing. It shouldn't hold. It held.

Bankwest decided to be the *just enough* bank (Bear Meets Eagle On Fire, 2025), which reads like an apology right up until you notice it's a boast.

KFC cut the three most famous words it owns down to *FLG* (Ogilvy, 2025).

And DiDi got *Yes, I DiDi* (Sunday Gravy, 2025), which kicks both ways exactly as Goodis demanded, and has been lodged in my head against my will ever since.

I had to look those credits up. I get a bit exhausted watching agencies say they do good work. I'd rather advertising just be advertising. But Pope credited everyone, so I can manage four.

Four, not five. I couldn't find who wrote *Special Buys*. It's Aldi's own label, and BMF have made the advertising around it here for years. Somebody named it. They're not in the trade press.

Each of those is a word put somewhere it had no business being, and then it can't be taken out. There's no name for that in Pope's list. There was no slot to put it in.

It's also the only part of this job I have never been able to explain to a client in advance.

## Mine

None of that work is mine, and I'd rather say so than have you work it out.

I've got three. Two of them you haven't heard of.

*Send Joy*, for Inke, who make custom packaging for other brands. It's on the box, and it's why you'd hire them to send yours.

*You-shaped property*, for Shape Property Group. Their name and their promise to the customer, in one hyphen.

And *24/7/7/11*, for 7-Eleven.

None of them came out of a method. Each one turned up, and then it was all I could think about.

## The part that doesn't automate

A language model is a next-likely-word engine. It is built to produce the expected continuation.

Rhetoric is pattern. Ask for twenty headlines using anesis and you'll get twenty, four of them will kick both ways, and it takes about eleven seconds. Pope's scales are the most automated thing in the building. The lifeboat floods first.

The idiosyncratic is the improbable word that turns out to be right. Which is the thing a probability engine is built not to do.

Has a model ever handed me one? No. That hasn't happened yet, with a model, at all. It will give you weird all day. It can't tell which weird is the one, because that isn't in the language. It's in the room, the country, the year, the way the word sits in the mouth of someone standing in an aisle in Preston on a Thursday.

Weird is cheap now. Right-weird never was.

I don't think you get that magic from a model. It has to be something closer to divine intervention, or desperation.

## She was right, for a reason that wasn't hers

Practise the scales. Rhetoric is the first thing to go, but you cannot find the improbable word without having written the forty expected ones first. The scales build the ear.

Stop writing the forty and you can't hear it. You pick the fourth-best line, confidently, on time, and nothing tells you.

I'm not above this. When the options are endless, knowing the right one gets difficult. There's no recreatable path to it.

I don't know yet whether my own ear is still being fed or just being spent. Fifteen years of bad headlines built it. I'm writing fewer bad ones now.

---

You can't recreate this computationally. I can't recreate it either.

Which is why I think creative brains are going to matter more. I would say that.

And everything else is against you. The market pressure. The stakeholder feedback. The client worried about getting all five things from the brief into three words.

It's not easy.

I did look, by the way. I still don't know who wrote *Special Buys*.
