Suzanne Pope wrote *An Inconvenient Truth for Copywriters* around 2010. Group creative director at john st. in Toronto, seventy-one pages, forty-odd ads, every agency credited. If you haven't read it, it's a craft document. A reminder to be a great writer, illustrated by people who were.

It gets described as a warning about copywriters going extinct. That's Part One, and she introduces Part One herself as "yet another lecture", a dead horse getting flogged, and tells you to scroll past it if you'd rather. Which is a fair indication of where she thought the value sat.

Part Two is the value. The tools of rhetoric: metaphor, personification, hyperbole, irony, paradox, puns, parallelism, anesis, simile, the rhetorical question. Two thousand years old and still amazingly sharp, she says. Practise them the way a musician practises scales. Never with the intention of performing them, but it's most of what makes a performance possible.

The best line in it isn't hers. She takes a layout with a particularly bad pun on it to Jerry Goodis (her first boss, and the most famous ad person in Canada through the sixties and seventies) and he hands it straight back. *If you're going to do a pun, it has to kick both ways.*

I've had that document on my drive for years. I've only just worked out what was bothering me about it.

## Look at what she chose

Nearly every ad in it is a visual idea.

The headline and the picture are doing it together, and in most cases neither half survives the other being taken away. She opens the metaphor section with a noisy child drawn as a loudspeaker. There's a bathroom fixture with a shocked face. There are the anti-gun posters. She is cataloguing writing, and she is doing it almost entirely with work where the words and the image are welded to each other.

That's not a flaw in her selection. It's the condition of the job, and it was the condition long before she wrote.

Copywriters have had to think visually since the sixties. Bernbach is the reason. He sat the writer and the art director at the same desk because the division wasn't real, and we have spent sixty years in pairs arguing about whether the line or the picture gets to carry it. Which is a completely different problem from words going extinct. It's a seating arrangement.

So the endangered-species thing was never the danger. She knew. She told you to skip it.

## The device is a slot

Here's the part that took me longer.

You can name the figure of speech in every one of those forty ads. Anesis. Paradox. Parallelism. You can name it correctly, and naming it tells you nothing at all about why the ad is good.

She uses a Martin Agency ad about Kennedy and the moon landing, where the line sets up a run of metaphors and then uses the last one literally. The device is real. You could brief that structure to ten writers, all ten would hand back the structure, and nine would hand back nothing.

The device is a slot. What goes in the slot is the entire job.

## Special Buys

Not "weekly deals". Not "limited offers".

*Special Buys.* Faintly wrong. Slightly foreign. "Buys" as a noun, which it barely is. And now load-bearing for an entire supermarket's personality in this country, to the point where people say it out loud in the aisle without embarrassment.

Nobody arrived at that by selecting a figure of speech.

CBA built years of work on *can* (M&C Saatchi, 2012). A verb, hung out on its own and used as a thing. Determined to can. It shouldn't hold. It held.

Bankwest decided to be the *just enough* bank (Bear Meets Eagle On Fire, 2025), which is the least bank-shaped sentiment available and reads like an apology right up until you notice it's a boast.

KFC took the three most famous words it owns and cut them down to *FLG* (Ogilvy, 2025). Seventy-odd years of slogan, abbreviated like something you'd thumb out in the drive-through queue. A legal department somewhere had to be talked into that.

And DiDi got *Yes, I DiDi* (Sunday Gravy, 2025), a line that kicks both ways precisely as Goodis demanded, attached to a flute-playing menace called Nudgy who has been lodged in my head against my will ever since.

I had to look those credits up, and I'd rather not have. I met every one of these the way you're supposed to meet an ad, by accident, on the way to something else. Reading agencies explain that their work is good wears me out, and an ad that needs the case study has usually already lost. But Pope credited everyone across seventy-one pages, so I can manage four.

Four, not five. I couldn't find who wrote *Special Buys*. It's Aldi's own label, older than any campaign I can trace, and BMF have made the advertising around it here for years. Somebody named it. They're not in the trade press.

Each of those is a word put somewhere it had no business being, and then it can't be taken out. The brand steps up a level and nothing in the process accounts for it. There's no device name for it, which is why it isn't in Pope's taxonomy. There was no slot to put it in.

It's also the only part of this job I have never been able to explain to a client in advance.

## Mine, since I'm handing out everyone else's

You'll have noticed that none of that work is mine. This whole piece is built on lines I didn't write, which is an uncomfortable place to argue from and I'd rather say so than have you work it out.

I've got three I'd put up. Two of them you haven't heard of.

*Send Joy*, for Inke, who make custom packaging for other brands. It's on the box, and it's the reason you'd hire them to send yours. Two words, kicks both ways, Goodis can have it back.

*You-shaped property*, for Shape Property Group. Their name and their promise to the customer, held together by a hyphen.

And *24/7/7/11*, which I'm not going to explain.

None of them came out of a method. Each one turned up, and then for about a week it was the only thing I could think about.

## The part that doesn't automate

A language model is a next-likely-word engine. That's not a slur, it's the mechanism. It is built to produce the expected continuation.

Rhetoric is pattern, and pattern is what it's made of. Ask for twenty headlines using anesis and you'll get twenty, four of them will kick both ways, and it takes about eleven seconds. Pope's scales are now the most thoroughly automated thing in the building. The lifeboat floods first.

The idiosyncratic is the improbable word that turns out to be right. The low-probability choice. Which is, structurally, the thing a probability engine is built not to do.

The obvious objection is that it must have happened by now. A model hands you an improbable word, it's the right one, you keep it.

It hasn't. Not once, not yet, and I've given it every chance. Ask a model for something weird and it will give you weird all day (it is extremely good at weird). What it can't do is know which improbable word is the one that makes a brand step up, because that isn't in the language. It's in the room, the country, the year, the way the word sits in the mouth of someone standing in an aisle in Preston on a Thursday. Forty things nobody wrote down.

Weird is cheap now. Right-weird never was.

When it has turned up for me it has felt like one of two things. Divine intervention, or desperation. I can't schedule either.

## She was right, for a reason that wasn't hers

Practise the scales. Rhetoric isn't the way out, it's the first thing to go. But you cannot find the improbable word without having written the forty expected ones first.

The scales aren't the weapon. They're how you build the ear that knows the weapon when it turns up.

Which means the risk was never a machine writing your headlines. The risk is that you stop writing the forty, and then you can't hear it. You pick the fourth-best line, confidently, on time, and nothing in the process tells you what just happened.

I'm not above this. Coming out of a pitch recently I could take a thought a long way on my own. Scamps, storyboards, treatments, the shape of the whole thing worked out while the idea was still moving. Genuinely useful. Also the part I'd watch, because it has never been easier to make a thought look finished before it's any good.

And the options don't end now. That's the bit I didn't see coming. When there were forty lines on the wall you could stand in front of them. When there are four hundred, and four hundred more if you ask, knowing which one is right gets harder. The right one is in there no more often than it used to be.

I don't know yet whether my own ear is still being fed or just being spent. Fifteen years of bad headlines built it. I'm writing fewer bad ones now.

## About the pictures

None of these ads ran. There is no Strong Jawman, no Orvant, no Velmora. Nobody craned that jaw onto a roof and nobody stood on the footpath with a camera.

I was going to repaint the famous ones and thought better of it, so these are their cousins. The cowboy who is mostly jaw. The car that proves something about the man beside it. The appliance with feelings. Each hangs off a device from her list: hyperbole, parallelism, personification, and a pun that kicks both ways.

A machine made all four, in the look of old slide film, for an essay about the part of the job a machine can't do. *Strong Jawman* is mine. The other three lines it pitched, in a list of twelve, and I picked.

That's either the joke or the whole argument, and I've stopped trying to separate them.

---

You can't get to that word by computation. I can't get to it by method either, which is the less flattering half. There's no path I could walk twice.

Which is why I think the people who can find it are going to matter more, not less. I would say that.

And everything around the line pushes the other way. The market. The stakeholder round. A client who, quite reasonably, wants all five things in the brief delivered in three words.

Five things. Three words. It's not easy.

I did look, by the way. I still don't know who wrote *Special Buys*.
