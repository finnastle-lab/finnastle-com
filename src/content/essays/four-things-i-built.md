A certificate only proves you sat still. That's the sentence I kept coming back to over the eight Google AI Professional Certificates I've spent the year working through, the last of which I added to my profile today. Sitting still isn't nothing. But it isn't the thing either, and I didn't want to be the guy who finishes a course and calls it a skill.

So I ran each one as a workshop instead of a syllabus. Built something real off the back of it, let it break, fixed what could be fixed, and paid attention to what couldn't. Four of those builds are worth writing down, because between them they answer the only question I actually care about — what happens to making when machines can make — better than the certificates themselves did.

The machine is very good at ordering things it doesn't understand. That's the [FA Brand Reference](/tools/design-system/) in one line. For years the Astle Creative visual system lived in my head — the monochrome-first palette, the three type voices in tension, the marks, which rules were load-bearing and which were decoration. Fine, until someone else needed to make something look like me. Or until a machine did. Writing the system down wasn't a design exercise, it was an admission: the only way to hand this off is to make it legible to something that has no taste of its own.

Which is the same instinct behind the [Icon Pack](/tools/icon-pack) — the second page of that reference, and the messier one. I had years of scanned collage elements sitting in folders named the way scanners name things, IMG_4471, scan_final_FINAL, useless the second you actually want a specific angel at 1am. So I had an LLM go through the raw material and rename everything into strict, searchable keywords. Now pulling an element into a new composition takes seconds. Here's the part I didn't expect to enjoy: the model is brilliant at the filing and hopeless at the making. It'll organise a decade of scans without complaint and can't produce a single decent dada collage on its own. The cognitive friction that makes a collage actually mine is the one thing it flattens every time it tries. Good. That's the boundary I was hoping to find, not the one I was worried about.

Some tasks don't need taste, they need a schedule. That's [Monthly Visual & Social Performance Studio](/tools), which isn't precious about anything — it pulls the performance data off my Instagram posts, reach and engagement and the image itself, and lands it in one place tied back to the art instead of floating loose as a number that means nothing by Tuesday. It runs itself every Sunday, which is the entire feature. The value isn't in a dashboard. It's that I can finally ask which work is worth making more of and get an answer built on a history instead of a vibe. It took a while to get there — I spent longer than I'd like to admit chasing a broken login token that turned out not to be the problem at all, which is its own lesson about trusting the obvious explanation over the actual error.

And then there's [DreadSwipe](/tools), which exists mostly out of spite for Letterboxd. Watching a film used to be a private thing; somewhere along the way it became a public one, ratings and reviews and the low-grade performance of having opinions about cinema in front of an audience. DreadSwipe strips that back to a gesture: swipe left to dismiss, right to watchlist, up for the high-intent super-like that pushes recommendations toward the strange and visceral and away from the mainstream noise. No stars. No takes. Just a fast yes or no, built on the TMDB API, for an audience of one.

None of these are impressive on their own. A renamed folder. A scheduled export. A swipe gesture. That's rather the point — the certificates didn't teach me to do anything dramatic, they taught me where the machine's competence actually ends, which turns out to be a much shorter list than the marketing suggests. It's excellent at retrieval, filing, scheduling, structuring. It is nowhere near the part where you decide what's actually worth making.

Eight certificates in, that's the whole return: not a new set of tricks, but a clearer line around the ones that were never going to be tricks at all.

Say g'day if any of these are useful to you. 🙏
